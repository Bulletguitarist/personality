Unit 1 · Introduction to Personality Psychology

What makes you, you?

"Knowing yourself is the beginning of all wisdom" — Aristotle. Personality is the relatively enduring pattern of thoughts, feelings and behaviours that makes each person's psychological fingerprint unique. This page walks through both your Unit 1 slides and the Freud deck, end to end.

Defining Personality

Allport alone gave around 50 definitions of personality — the two most cited for exams:

LARSEN & BUSS, 2005

"Personality is the set of psychological traits and mechanisms within the individual that are organized and relatively enduring and that influence his or her interactions with, and adaptations to, the intrapsychic, physical, and social environments."

SCHULTZ & SCHULTZ, 2015

"Personality is an enduring and unique cluster of characteristics that may change in response to different situations."

  • Personality is unique to the individual, yet relatively stable — it can still develop over time.
  • It shapes thoughts, emotions and behaviour, and understanding it improves communication, relationships, career success and self-awareness.
  • Academic definitions of personality differ sharply from popular/everyday definitions — exams often test this distinction.
1.2

Temperament, Trait & Personality

Psychologists treat these as distinct, layered concepts — visualised with the "House Analogy."

TEMPERAMENT — THE FOUNDATION
Soil you build on

Biologically-based, innate, present from infancy. Basic tendencies: activity, regularity, initial reaction, adaptability, intensity, mood, distractibility, persistence-attention span, sensory threshold.

TRAIT — THE PILLARS
Walls you build up

A label for a combination of behaviours; moderately stable, specific and measurable (e.g. extraversion, neuroticism, sensation seeking, openness, conscientiousness); develops with age.

PERSONALITY — THE HOME
Complete, furnished home

The comprehensive set of characteristics and patterns of thoughts, emotions and behaviours — the paint, furniture, habits and daily life inside. Dynamic; evolves over time.

The Big Five (OCEAN) — psychology's gold standard for traits

Openness

Curious vs. cautious

Conscientiousness

Organized vs. easygoing

Extraversion

Outgoing vs. reserved

Agreeableness

Compassionate vs. competitive

Neuroticism: sensitive/anxious vs. secure/confident. Someone who scores exceptionally high in Conscientiousness will consistently keep an immaculate calendar, arrive early, and feel deeply uncomfortable with disorganised, last-minute plans.

1.3

How Personality Shows Up in Behaviour

When is a trait activated?

Specific situations allow expression or suppression of traits — e.g. a library, college, a satsang, or an emergency each pull different traits forward.

Consistency of personality traits — a long-debated topic

Tested via honesty in exams, paying taxes, relationships — traits are consistent enough to be labelled "someone's personality" (relatively consistent). What a person self-reports and what others observe tend to be more or less similar.

Personality as a lens

Personality actively shapes how you filter and react to the world — driving your view of self, others and the world, your reactions to situations, your interactions, your feelings, and ultimately your selection of friends, partner and career, and how you shape your life through your actions.

1.5

Current Research Trends

Personality differences across cultures, ages, political ideologies, gender and socioeconomic groups are major areas of current interest.

Personality & Social Media

Core questions: is the personality you see online the same as real life? Does social media use shape personality, or just reveal it differently?

  • Online personalities are as accurate as face-to-face impressions (Gosling, Gaddis & Vazire, 2007; Back et al., 2010).
  • People present as more emotionally stable online than they are in reality (Blumer & Döring, 2012).
  • Introverted, neurotic, lonely and socially awkward individuals find it easier to express their true selves online (Marriott & Buchanan, 2014).
  • High social media/internet use is linked to increased neurotic tendencies, lower psychological well-being and lower self-esteem (Seidman, 2014; Kross et al., 2013).
  • Neurotic individuals passively use social media, engage in social comparison and heightened FOMO, using it to cope with distress — which disrupts wellbeing further (Rozgonjuk et al., 2019).
  • Heavy social-network users skew more extraverted, more open, but lower in self-esteem, socialisation skills, conscientiousness and emotional stability.

Gender Differences in Personality

  • Neuroticism: emotional complexity, anxiety, agreeableness greater in women; anger/hostility greater in men.
  • Extraversion: women score higher on warmth, gregariousness, positive emotions; men score higher on assertiveness and excitement seeking (Feingold, 1994; Costa et al., 2001).
  • Openness: women score higher on aesthetics/feelings facets; men score higher on the ideas facet.
  • The "why" is unclear — possible reasons: evolution, social roles, cultural norms.

Culture & Personality

  • Cultural environments shape/reinforce traits through socialisation — but the reverse causation hypothesis holds too: shared personality dispositions can shape the culture people build (systematic, bidirectional interaction).
  • Parenting: individualistic US culture → noncoercive, democratic, permissive parenting; collectivist Asian/Arab cultures → more authoritarian, restrictive, controlling — shaping different personality outcomes.
  • High Individualism cultures → higher Extraversion & Openness; high Power Distance → lower Agreeableness.
  • High Uncertainty Avoidance → higher Conscientiousness, lower Openness. Agricultural societies → higher Conscientiousness/Agreeableness. Urbanised cultures → higher Openness/Extraversion.
Framework · Geert Hofstede

Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions

A framework explaining how a society's culture shapes the values and behaviour of its members — 6 key dimensions.

POWER DISTANCE (PDI)
Hierarchy acceptance

High PDI: strict hierarchies, rarely questioned (Malaysia, Mexico). Low PDI: flat structures, open challenge to authority (Denmark, Israel).

INDIVIDUALISM vs COLLECTIVISM (IDV)
"I" vs "We"

High IDV: merit-based, personal goals (US, Australia). Low IDV/Collectivist: in-group loyalty, "losing face" matters (Guatemala, South Korea).

MASCULINITY vs FEMININITY (MAS)
Competition vs quality of life

High MAS: competition, achievement, long hours (Japan, Slovakia). High Femininity: work-life balance, modesty (Sweden, Norway).

UNCERTAINTY AVOIDANCE (UAI)
Comfort with ambiguity

High UAI: rigid rules, detailed contracts (Greece, Japan). Low UAI: comfortable with ambiguity, "failing fast" (Singapore, UK).

LONG- vs SHORT-TERM ORIENTATION (LTO)
Future planning vs immediate results

High LTO: long-range R&D, high savings (China, South Korea). Low LTO: quarterly results focus (Nigeria, US).

INDULGENCE vs RESTRAINT (IND)
Gratification vs discipline

High Indulgence: leisure, festivals prioritised (Mexico, Colombia). High Restraint: disciplined emotional expression (Egypt, Russia).

Workplace takeaway: cultural sensitivity in recruitment and communication promotes DEI; training programmes help build interpersonal skills for multicultural teams and manage acculturation.

1.6

Biological Influences on Personality

Genes & Personality

  • The controversy: behavioural genetics debates genes vs. environment. In 1996, a gene was linked to "novelty seeking" behaviour tied to extraversion/sensation seeking — raising questions about "designer babies" and eugenics (improving the species by selective reproduction).
  • Ethical/ideological risk: ignoring environment's role, misuse for political purposes (e.g. "master race" ideology).
  • Finding the golden spot: knowledge beats ignorance for parenting practices — a partly genetic trait can still be managed or changed.

Key findings

  • Sensation/Novelty seeking: the 1996 study linked the dopamine receptor gene DRD4 to thrill-seeking.
  • Extraversion & Neuroticism: twin studies show ~40–60% heritability.
  • Aggression & psychopathy: fearlessness, manipulation, low empathy show moderate heritability — environment shapes whether it leads to crime or acceptable outlets (e.g. high-stakes business).
  • Propensity to marry: surprisingly heritable (~68%) — likely linked to sociability/agreeableness.
  • Substance abuse: alcohol dependence is more heritable than casual drinking.

Shared vs. non-shared environment

Shared environments (family, school, household rules) have surprisingly little impact on most traits — adopted siblings raised together are barely similar in personality. Bigger effects come from nonshared environments — unique friends, teachers, experiences. Exceptions: attitudes, political/religious beliefs, health habits (smoking) and antisocial behaviour/depression — where shared environment does matter.

Physiological Basis of Personality

  • Old physiological classifications (Ayurveda, Hippocrates — dominant bodily fluids) are now outdated; modern neuroscience uses fMRI.
  • Canli et al. (2001): high-neuroticism scorers showed more frontal-brain activity to negative images; high-extraversion scorers showed more activity to positive images.
  • Testosterone ↑ uninhibited/aggressive/risk-taking behaviour. Shy children show higher cortisol (more stress). MAO helps regulate neurotransmitters, influencing thrill-seeking.
  • Introverts vs. extraverts (Eysenck/Gale, 1987): introverts show stronger, quicker nervous-system responses to moderate stimulation (more physiologically reactive); in low-stimulation settings introverts stay more alert, extraverts get bored; in high-stimulation settings extraverts perform better as introverts become overaroused.
1.7

Evolutionary Influences on Personality

HOGAN'S THEORY (1983)
Status & acceptance

The two most basic human motivators: gaining status and achieving group acceptance. Early humans had to build cooperative relationships and navigate hierarchies — status/popularity meant protection, food, mates; exclusion was harmful to survival.

BAUMEISTER & TICE (1990)
Social anxiety

Evolved as an adaptation to prevent exclusion. Without social anxiety, people risk exclusion, lost protection, fewer mates and lower reproductive success.

BAUMEISTER & LEARY (1995)
Need to belong

A central human motivation — groups provide food, protection, mates, and kin support. Social pain activates the same brain regions as physical pain (anterior cingulate cortex) — MacDonald & Leary (2005).

PARENTAL INVESTMENT THEORY (TRIVERS, 1972)
Sex differences

Females invest more in offspring (pregnancy, lactation); males compete more intensely for high-investing females — driving male risk-taking, aggression, and male-on-male violence.

Evolutionary jealousy asymmetry

Because fertilization is internal, men risk investing in offspring that aren't theirs (paternal uncertainty) — women never face this. Predicted responses: men are more upset by sexual infidelity (threatens paternity certainty); women are more upset by emotional infidelity (threatens resources/long-term support).

1.8

Environmental Factors of Personality

Parenting styles

Authoritative

Warm, structured — linked to high self-esteem, social competence, lower neuroticism.

Authoritarian

Strict, less warmth — elevated neuroticism, lower openness/extraversion/self-esteem (varies cross-culturally).

Permissive / indulgent

Mixed outcomes — less consistent than authoritative; sometimes positive in European/Latin populations.

Uninvolved / neglectful

Nearly universally linked to low conscientiousness/agreeableness, high neuroticism, poorer social adjustment.

Other environmental factors

  • Family environment: high conflict/low cohesion → anxiety, depression, reduced resilience; nurturing environments → secure attachment, emotional regulation.
  • Neighbourhood: safety, cohesion, resources shape risk exposure and stress coping.
  • Critical life events: moderate stress/trauma can spark post-traumatic growth — improved resilience, confidence, mastery — especially with positive meaning-making.
  • Schools: socialisation, identity formation; peer/teacher feedback shapes self-esteem, conscientiousness, social competence.
  • Peer groups: shape norms, identity exploration, risk-taking, prosocial engagement.
  • Media: cultivates self-image and social comparison; influencer culture can lower self-esteem via upward comparison.
1.9

What Makes a Good Theory?

A theory is a systematic set of ideas explaining, predicting and helping us understand a phenomenon based on evidence. It cannot be "right or wrong" — only more or less aligned with scientific observation. Functions: describe, explain, predict, control.

Components of personality theories

  • Structure — what personality is made of.
  • Motivation — why people behave as they do.
  • Development — how personality develops across life.
  • Assessment — how personality is measured.
  • Psychopathology — how the theory explains abnormal behaviour.
  • Personality change — how personality can change.

1.10

Basic Assumptions About Human Nature

Every personality theory rests on assumptions about human beings — 8 key dichotomies.

Free Will vs. Determinism
Free willBehaviour is based on personal choice. Eg: a student chooses to study daily despite distractions. Supported by Rogers, Maslow.
DeterminismBehaviour is shaped by biology, environment, childhood experiences or unconscious motives. Eg: anxiety from traumatic childhood. Supported by Freud, Skinner.
Nature vs. Nurture
NatureGenetics — intelligence, temperament, emotional sensitivity. Eg: identical twins show similar traits.
NurtureEnvironment — family, school, friends, culture. Eg: a supportive family builds confidence.
Stability vs. Change
StabilityPersonality stays relatively constant. Eg: a shy child stays shy as an adult. Trait theorists support this.
ChangePersonality can shift through experience. Eg: a timid employee grows confident after leadership training. Humanistic/social learning theories support this.
Optimism vs. Pessimism
OptimisticHumans are naturally good, capable of growth. Eg: learning from failure builds resilience. Rogers, Maslow.
PessimisticBehaviour driven by inner conflict, aggression, selfish motives. Eg: kindness mainly to avoid guilt. Freud.
Conscious vs. Unconscious Behaviour
ConsciousAware of thoughts/feelings/actions. Eg: studying because you want to score well.
UnconsciousBehaviour driven by thoughts outside awareness. Eg: avoiding public speaking due to forgotten childhood embarrassment. Freud.
Rationality vs. Irrationality
RationalLogical, reasoned decisions. Eg: comparing job offers before accepting one.
IrrationalEmotions/impulses/unconscious motives drive behaviour. Eg: an impulsive expensive purchase despite financial difficulty.
Holism vs. Reductionism
HolismUnderstand the whole person — thoughts, emotions, behaviour, environment together. Humanistic psychology.
ReductionismUnderstand via smaller components — genes, brain processes, learned behaviours. Eg: a behaviourist explains aggression as a learned, reinforced response.
Individuality vs. Universality
IndividualityEvery person's personality and experience is unique. Eg: siblings raised together develop different personalities.
UniversalityHumans share common psychological characteristics. Eg: most people experience happiness, fear, sadness.
Part 2

Sigmund Freud: Classical Psychoanalysis

The oldest, most foundational perspective in personality psychology — the psychodynamic approach.

2.1

Freud's Life & the Psychodynamic Approach

"Psychodynamic" refers to understanding behaviour through the role of unconscious thoughts, feelings and memories — the oldest perspective on personality, built on the work of Sigmund Freud (Psychodynamic/Psychoanalytic approach).

  • Freud was born in an Austrian town, now part of Czechoslovakia. He was a Jew and faced persecution as Hitler rose to power.
  • Ernst Brucke: living organisms are dynamic systems obeying laws of the physical universe — an early influence on Freud.
  • Basically a physician, he worked extensively on "hysteria" — now known as conversion reactions.
  • Trained under Jean Charcot (treated hysteria via hypnotic suggestion) and Josef Breuer (success with hysterical patients by encouraging free talk about symptoms).
  • Ernest Jones wrote his biography, The Life and Work of Sigmund Freud.
  • Founded The Psychological Wednesday Society, later renamed the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society.

Freud's view of human nature

Animalistic & primordial

Selfish motives; sexual and aggressive desires drive behaviour.

Role of the unconscious

Most of what drives us lies outside conscious awareness.

Key points of focus in the psychoanalytic approach

Unconscious mind → Repression of true feelings → Awareness of true feelings.

2.2 · Topographical Model

Freud's Concept of Mind

Also called the Levels of Consciousness model — pictured as an iceberg.

Conscious mind10% — visible above water
Preconscious mind
Unconscious mind90% — hidden beneath
CONSCIOUS MIND
Currently aware of

Things you're aware of right now. Eg: what is happening around you at present.

PRECONSCIOUS MIND
Available memory

Things you can recollect with some effort. Eg: what you ate for yesterday's lunch; how you celebrated your birthday last year.

UNCONSCIOUS MIND
Suppressed & forgotten

Things you've suppressed and don't want to remember — forgotten due to painful emotions attached. Eg: painful childhood memories.

Contents of the unconscious include: present thoughts, perceptions (near-conscious), and — deeper down — aggressive wishes, sexual instincts, selfish desires, shameful experiences, fears, painful memories, painful childhood memories, unacceptable wishes.

2.3 · The Anatomy of Personality

The Structural Model

The tripartite division of personality: Id, Ego and Superego.

ID
Pleasure Principle

"The id is a kid." Instinctive, oldest structure of mind — raw, animalistic, chaotic. Immediate discharge of psychic energy from biologically-rooted drives; if unexpressed, creates tension. Impulsive, irrational, narcissistic — doesn't recognise fear/anxiety or consequences.

EGO
Reality Principle

Decision-making component; ensures safety and self-preservation. Battles for survival against both the external world and instinctual demands. Releases id's raw energy within social bounds. Rational, cognitive — the "executive of personality."

SUPEREGO
Morality Principle

Acquired through socialisation — no one is born with it. Develops via parents, teachers and formative figures from ~3–5 years. Represents right-wrong, good-bad; fully developed once self-control replaces parental control. Guides toward perfectionistic goals.

Cathexis & Anti-cathexis — distribution of psychic energy

  • Cathexis: investment of energy to fulfil an instinct — can be shifted via displacement.
  • Id: identification (modelling oneself after others), ego-cathexis (investing in a realistic goal), wishful thinking (fantasising), anti-cathexis (suppressing).
  • Ego: conscience uses anti-cathexis against ego and id, follows the moral principle, and tries to suspend both pleasure and reality principles to do what's right.
  • Anti-cathexis: preventing gratification of an instinct.
2.4

Instincts: Eros & Thanatos

EROS — LIFE INSTINCT
Survival & pleasure

Basic survival, pleasure, reproduction — essential for sustaining the individual and the species. Associated with love, cooperation, prosocial actions. Its energy is called libido / libidinal energy. Governed by erogenous zones.

THANATOS — DEATH INSTINCT
"The goal of all life is death"

An unconscious desire to die, tempered by life instincts. People who experience trauma often reenact it — an instinctive "pressure toward death." Linked to fear, hate, anger and anti-social acts (bullying to murder).

Both instincts operate under the Law of Conservation of Energy.

The four features of any instinct

1. Source

The bodily condition or need from which it arises.

2. Aim

To abolish the excitation from that need — achieving it gives a momentary blissful state.

3. Object

A person/thing providing satisfaction — the choice of objects is flexible and changes across life.

4. Impetus

The magnitude of energy used to satisfy the instinct.

2.5

Anxiety

A consequence of inadequate discharge of libido.

NEUROTIC ANXIETY

Threat that unacceptable id impulses will become conscious; fear of severe consequences from doing something terrible; occurs when id impulses threaten to break through ego controls.

MORAL ANXIETY

Ego threatened by punishment from superego — shame, guilt, self-condemnation over expressing id. Objective fear of parental punishment; fear of exclusion, death, afterlife punishment.

REALISTIC ANXIETY

Emotional response to real, perceived external dangers. Terminates as the threat subsides — self-preservation; helps cope with danger.

2.6

Ego Defense Mechanisms

A way of reducing anxiety by blocking or distorting reality — a mental strategy against open id expression and opposing superego pressures. They operate unconsciously, are self-deceptive, and either block or distort the impulse to reduce its intensity.

Denial

Blocking external events from awareness — refusing to experience overwhelming situations despite evidence. Most typical of young children/immature adults.

Repression

Excluding distressing thoughts/feelings from consciousness. Remains active in the unconscious, requiring constant psychic energy to suppress. Shows up in dreams, jokes, slips of the tongue.

Projection

Attributing unacceptable internal thoughts/feelings/behaviours to others or the environment — blaming someone/something for your own shortcomings.

Displacement

Redirecting impulses from a more threatening object/person to something less threatening.

Rationalization

Fallacious reasoning that turns irrational behaviour into seemingly rational behaviour — the "sour grapes" pattern.

Reaction Formation

Expressing the opposite of the original (repressed) thought. First: an unwanted impulse is repressed into the unconscious. Second: its exact opposite is expressed consciously.

Sublimation

A healthy, constructive, socially-acceptable strategy for channelling objectionable impulses (e.g. into art).

2.7 · Personality Development

The Psychosexual Stages

0–18 months
Oral Stage
Mouth: sucking, chewing, biting — first zone of tension-reducing pleasure. Weaning & separation from mother. Libido attaches to the oral zone (nail-biting, smoking, overeating). Difficulty giving up the breast → more libido stuck here, less for later stages. Frustration/overindulgence: Oral passive (cheerful, optimistic, gullible, dependent) vs. Oral aggressive (argumentative, sarcastic, exploitative, dominating).
18 months – 3 yrs
Anal Stage
Anus: retaining/expelling faeces, toilet training. First exposure to id-superego conflict — the child learns their body is a "weapon" against parents. Anal expulsive/aggressive: disorderly, hostile, impulsive, destructive. Anal retentive: ordered, obstinate, stingy, rigid, compulsively neat. Later self-control and mastery originate here.
3–6 yrs
Phallic Stage
Genitals are the focus of libido. Task: identifying with the same-sex role model. Children explore/manipulate genitals, become curious about anatomical differences, may express wanting to marry the opposite-sex parent. The most complex conflicts to resolve — involve the incest taboo. Boys: Oedipus complex. Girls: Electra complex. Phallic personality: strong narcissism, needs continual admiration; can show impotency, frigidity, infidelity.
6–12 yrs
Latency Stage
By age 5, id/ego/superego structures are formed and solidifying. Not technically a psychosexual stage — the sex instinct is dormant, temporarily sublimated into school activities, hobbies, sports, and same-sex friendships.
Puberty onward
Genital Stage
The final stage — body becomes physiologically mature. If no major earlier fixations, the individual can lead a normal life; Freud believed conflict here is less intense. Sexual energy is satisfied through socially acceptable substitutes and, later, committed adult relationships. Satisfaction is found in love and work — work being an acceptable sublimation outlet.

Oedipus Complex (boys)

Named after Sophocles' Oedipus Rex. Mother becomes the love object; father is seen as a rival, feared for his power — leading to castration anxiety. Resolved around 5–7 years: the boy represses desire for mother and identifies with father — this identification is the origin of the superego. Fixation → brash, boastful, reckless masculinity.

Electra Complex (girls)

From Sophocles' Electra, who had her brother kill their mother. The girl realises she lacks a penis (penis envy), blames the mother, feels hostility, wishes for the father's protection, and later identifies with the mother, wishing to marry someone like her father. Fixation → exaggerated femininity used to overwhelm/conquer men.

2.8 · Application

Dream Interpretation

Application areas of psychoanalytic theory: psychoanalytic therapy, base of psychodynamic therapies, projective assessment techniques, and dream interpretation.

  • Manifest content: the actual symbol/imagery in the dream. Latent content: its underlying psychological meaning.
  • Dreams reveal conflicts in condensed, intensified form — any dream event can have many sources; some origins are simply mundane.
  • A Hong Kong study found people who slept prone (on stomach) more often dreamed of sexual activity, being smothered, locked up, tied, unable to move (Yu, 2012).
  • A large German study found the five most typical dreams involve: flying, trying something repeatedly, being chased, sexual experiences, and school-related matters (Göritz, 2014).

A modern perspective on common dream themes

  • Falling: loss of control, insecurity, fear of failure — anxieties about power, sex or instability.
  • Flying: freedom, escape, ambition — possibly sexual release or overcoming limitations.
  • Being chased: avoidance of an issue/confrontation — repressed fears or guilt.
  • Teeth falling out: anxiety about appearance/communication/loss — castration anxiety or fear of aging.
  • Water: emotions and the subconscious — sexuality or emotional states.
  • Nudity in public: vulnerability, shame, fear of exposure — hidden insecurities or repression.
  • Sexual dreams: desire, intimacy, unresolved sexual tension.
  • Death: change, transformation, fear of loss — suppressed aggression or wish for independence.
  • Being trapped: feeling stuck in a situation/relationship — repression of desires.
2.9

Evaluation of the Theory

STRENGTHS

First organized theory of personality; highly applicable; based on extensive clinical observation of neurotic patients plus self-analysis.

LIMITATIONS

Comparatively less empirical validity; needs refinement in the modern context; needs more sophisticated research methods for scientific rigor.
